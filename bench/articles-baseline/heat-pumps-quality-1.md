# Yes, Heat Pumps Work in Cold Climates — Here's the Evidence and the Limits

In field tests in Finland, a Mitsubishi model and a Toshiba model both held a COP above 2 down to -20°C. At -30°C, the Mitsubishi managed between 1.5 and 2, the Toshiba between 1 and 1.5. [1] COP is units of heat delivered per unit of electricity used.

That is evidence for two models in those tests. It does not settle how another heat pump will perform in your house.

## The Efficiency Evidence at Low Temperatures

Researchers plotted average COP against outdoor temperature from seven field studies in five countries. [2] For standard air-source heat pumps in mild cold climates, where average January temperatures sit above -10°C, average COPs ran between 2 and 3.

A 2021 study followed 10 Maine homes that used heat pumps as their primary heat. Seven used no supplemental heat all winter, and all 10 households reported being "satisfied" or "very satisfied" with comfort. [3] Ten homes cannot show how often that result holds. The source does not say how cold that winter got or how the homes were chosen.

A COP above 1 also says nothing about the bill. None of the sources compare electricity and fuel prices, tariffs, or installation costs, so they cannot settle whether an installation is cheaper to run than the boiler it replaces.

## The Technology Behind Cold-Weather Performance

A Massachusetts guide calls the variable-speed, inverter-driven vapor-injection compressor the distinguishing technology of a cold-climate heat pump. [4] The US Department of Energy defines the category by performance instead: greater capacity and efficiency below 32°F than traditional heat pumps. [5]

One maker claims its heat pump performs reliably down to -25°C. [6] An operating limit is not full output at that temperature, nor evidence of long-term reliability. Cold-climate systems in the field studies reached COPs above 1.5 down to -30°C [1], at whatever output they could still deliver.

## Real-World Proof: The Nordic Adoption Story

Two-thirds of households in Norway have a heat pump, the highest rate in the world, and Norway, Sweden, and Finland lead in heat pumps per 1,000 households. [7] Oslo has a design winter temperature of -20°C and recorded -29°C in 2010. [8]

That shows wide ownership where winters are cold. It does not show output on the coldest night or how much backup heat those homes use. Prices, incentives, and building stock could also drive adoption.

## The Honest Limits: Capacity Loss, Defrost, and Backup Heat

Below freezing, efficiency falls as an air-source heat pump extracts heat from colder air. [9] Capacity falls too, and capacity is the harder limit. Efficiency Manitoba says a cold-climate unit should have a COP of at least 1.8 at -15°C, and warns that heating capacity drops as outdoor temperature drops. [10] The house needs more heat as it gets colder while the pump supplies less. COP alone cannot tell you whether those curves cross in your house.

Defrost interrupts heating. Auxiliary electric resistance strips or a furnace can add heat indoors. [11] Whether that happens, and how often, depends on the system and the frosting conditions. If resistance backup supplies part of the heat, the combined COP is lower, and the sources do not give it.

Backup is needed or not depending on the house's heat load at its design temperature and the specific unit's output there. Seven of the 10 Maine homes used none. [3] Ask an installer what this unit delivers, in kilowatts and in COP including defrost, at the coldest temperature your house is designed for, and what covers any gap.
